What the receiver actually measures

A satellite broadcasts its position and the moment it transmitted. The receiver subtracts that moment from its own clock reading at arrival and multiplies by the speed of light. Call that ρi = |p − si| + c·b, where p is where you are and b is your clock's error. Light covers about 300 km per millisecond, so a quartz clock that is off by one millisecond shifts every circle by 300 km.

Why the clock becomes an unknown

Because the clock error shifts every circle by the same amount, it can be treated as one more unknown, solved at the same time as position. This page does exactly that with Levenberg–Marquardt least squares, in solver.js. In 2D the unknowns are x, y and b, so you need 3 satellites. Real GPS solves in 3D (x, y, z, b), so it needs 4.

Why not just build a better clock?

The satellites carry atomic clocks; your phone would cost far more with one. Instead, one extra satellite buys you atomic-clock accuracy for the price of a quartz crystal.

Geometry matters (HDOP)

Add timing noise, then drag the satellites close together. The circles cross at shallow angles, the intersection smears out, and HDOP climbs. The same noise now costs you far more position error.

One more thing

Satellite clocks run fast by about 38 microseconds a day compared with clocks on the ground (gravity slows ground clocks more than orbital speed slows satellite clocks). Uncorrected, that is roughly 11 km of drift per day, which is why the satellite clocks are deliberately tuned.

Toy scale: the map is about 3,000 km wide and the satellites are drawn close so the circles fit. Real GPS satellites orbit around 20,000 km up. The mechanism, however, is the real one.
